# Cat Care

Use this skill when the user asks about cat behavior, new-cat adaptation, feeding/drinking, grooming, household hazards, vocalizations, bonding, or whether a cat behavior is normal.

## Response style for this user

- Keep answers calm, practical, and reassuring, especially for a newly adopted cat.
- Lead with the likely interpretation, then give concrete next actions.
- Distinguish **normal adaptation behavior** from **warning signs**.
- Avoid over-medicalizing normal behaviors, but be explicit about urgent symptoms.
- When the cat is newly adopted or hiding, emphasize: do not force contact; let the cat choose distance; use predictable routines.

## New/adopted cat adaptation

1. Normalize early hiding and cautious exploration:
   - 3–7 days of hiding can be normal.
   - Shy cats may need 2–4 weeks or longer.
2. Core checks matter more than friendliness:
   - eating
   - drinking
   - urinating/defecating
   - night activity/exploration
3. Set up a stable safe zone:
   - food, water, litter box, hiding box/bed
   - keep positions stable
   - avoid frequent cleaning/moving of the hiding area early on
4. Human interaction pattern:
   - sit nearby and ignore the cat rather than stare/reach
   - speak softly
   - slow blink
   - toss treats between human and cat, gradually shortening distance
   - do not chase, grab, or force petting
5. If the cat approaches/rubs/head-butts:
   - good sign: scent marking and trust-building
   - keep movement slow and low
   - offer chin, cheek, and behind-ear scratches
   - avoid belly, paws, tail, and forced holding

## Behavior interpretation quick map

- Hiding in corners: usually fear/adjustment; check resources and give space.
- Running around at night/exploring: often progress from fear to territory-mapping.
- Long, sharp meows in a new home: may be calling for previous home/people, seeking reassurance, or checking resources.
- Approaches then runs when human moves: wants contact but is still unsure; lower body posture and reduce sudden movement.
- Head rubbing on hand/arm: affiliative scent marking; strong positive trust signal.
- Purring/low “motor” sound: comfort or self-soothing; interpret with body language.
- Chattering/clicking while staring at moving target: hunting excitement.
- Tail sweeping low on floor: emotional arousal; slow = cautious/uncertain, fast = irritation or overstimulation.
- Playing with curtains: exploration/hunting/climbing impulse; prioritize physical prevention (tie/clip curtains up, secure cords/loops), provide a legal vertical/抓 target near the window, redirect with play, and avoid chasing/spraying as punishment.
- Sitting/lying near stairs in a multi-floor home: often an observation post and territory boundary; normal if eating/drinking/litter use are normal. Do not force the cat downstairs; make lower floors safer with hiding boxes/owner-scent cloth and keep stairs clear/non-slip.
- Using litter on one floor but not lingering there: normal during adaptation — the cat may treat that floor as a necessary resource zone, not a safe living zone yet. If the kitten is young/small, consider a temporary second litter box near the safe zone to prevent accidents.
- Brief open-mouth “staring/sniffing” after smelling something: often Flehmen/vomeronasal scent analysis; distinguish from true open-mouth breathing/panting, which is urgent if prolonged, noisy, with fast chest/abdominal effort, lethargy, or occurs at rest.
- Post-zoomies panting: if a kitten has just been sprinting/playing hard, brief tongue-out open-mouth breathing can be exercise/heat excitement, especially in long-haired kittens. Tell the user to stop play, reduce stimulation, cool/ventilate the room, and watch recovery: it should noticeably ease and return to closed-mouth nasal breathing within ~1–3 minutes. Escalate to a vet/urgent care if it persists 3–5+ minutes, happens with little/no exertion, recurs often, or includes noisy/labored breathing, fast abdominal effort, blue/white/gray gums/tongue, collapse, lethargy, coughing/choking sounds, or heavy drooling.
- Biting cables: common play/chewing behavior but dangerous; immediately manage environment.

## Household safety for curious cats

Prioritize physical prevention over scolding.

- Cables: unplug when possible, use cable sleeves/conduits, cable boxes, ties, and keep dangling lines off the floor.
- Windows/curtains: secure windows/screens; tie up curtain cords/loops; prevent climbing hazards and falling curtain rods.
- Small objects: remove swallowable objects, rubber bands, twist ties, plastic bits.
- Appliances: keep washer/dryer/cabinets checked before closing.
- Coffee/tea/chocolate/energy drinks: caffeine is dangerous/toxic; prevent access to coffee grounds, coffee liquid, tea, and residue.
- Milk: ordinary cow milk is not recommended; many cats are lactose intolerant.
- Bread: plain white bread/toast in a tiny accidental bite is usually not toxic, but it is nutritionally unnecessary and should not become a treat. Check ingredients carefully: grape/raisin, chocolate, onion/garlic, xylitol/low-sugar sweeteners, alcohol/fermented dough, heavy cream/butter/cheese fillings are red flags. Advise removing access and monitoring 24h for vomiting, soft stool, lethargy, or appetite changes.
- Why cats eat nutritionally useless human foods: curiosity, smell (yeast/butter/milk/fat), texture, copying the human eating it, teething/exploration, or hunger. Emphasize “wants it” ≠ “needs it”; redirect to legal treats/toys.
- Recommended drink: fresh water; use wet food plus water to increase hydration if needed.

## Feeding basics

- Kittens: usually 3–4 small meals/day; more frequent small automated dry-food meals are also fine when total daily calories are controlled.
- Automatic feeder scheduling: if a machine dispenses fixed portions, divide the daily dry-food allowance into evenly spaced meals and, for kittens, put one meal late evening/midnight to reduce overnight hunger. Example: 50g/day with a 10g portion size = 5 meals/day such as 08:00, 12:00, 16:00, 20:00, 00:00. If wet food is added, subtract its calories from dry-food total instead of blindly keeping the same grams.
- Adults: usually 2 meals/day; 3 smaller meals can be gentler.
- Newly adopted/hiding cat:
  - leave some dry food where it can eat safely if appropriate
  - offer wet food at predictable times
  - check overnight intake and litter box use
  - avoid abrupt diet changes during stress unless medically needed
- Underweight kitten guidance:
  - For a 4-month kitten, very low weight should prompt focus on steady weight gain, appetite, stool quality, parasite status, and vet check/fecal test if growth stalls.
  - Recommend weekly same-time weigh-ins; prioritize kitten food/wet food and stable hydration over human foods.
- Human foods/dairy:
  - Plain unsweetened yogurt is not a necessary cat food and may cause soft stool/diarrhea; for young/light kittens, advise avoiding it or only a tiny taste with 24h stool monitoring.
  - Avoid sweetened/flavored yogurt, xylitol, chocolate/coffee/matcha flavors, yogurt drinks, and cow milk.
- For underweight kittens, prioritize trend over a single number: weigh weekly at a consistent time, watch appetite/energy/stool, and escalate if weight does not rise or drops.
- A 4-month kitten around 0.7 kg is notably light for many breeds/lines; if confirmed age is accurate, recommend vet assessment, fecal test, parasite check, and nutrition review rather than assuming normal small size.

## Deworming / parasite basics

- Ask what was given last time: medication name, date, whether it covered internal/external parasites, and dose by weight.
- If a newly adopted kitten was “dewormed before arrival” but details are unclear, avoid immediate repeat dosing without checking because small kittens have narrow dosing margins.
- Typical broad rhythm: kittens 3–6 months often need monthly internal parasite control; many external parasite preventives are monthly, but product labels and local vet guidance matter.
- For very light kittens, first deworming under the user's care is best done through a veterinarian rather than a pet shop: accurate weight, minimum-age/weight checks, fecal testing, and correct dosing are the key risks.
- Pet shops can help with simple product application, but they usually cannot replace medical judgment for underweight kittens, soft stool, suspected parasites, or uncertain prior treatment history.

## Grooming basics

- Licking fur is mainly cleaning, but also regulates temperature, restores personal scent, organizes fur, and self-soothes.
- Tail grooming is normal, including tail base and tip.
- Watch for overgrooming: repeated licking/biting one spot, hair loss, redness, scabs, tail-base chewing, flea dirt, pain response.

## Medical / urgent thresholds

Advise contacting a vet promptly if:

- no food for >24 hours
- no water for >24–36 hours
- no urine/defecation for ~48 hours
- repeated vomiting/diarrhea, severe lethargy, labored breathing, drooling, tremors, seizures, collapse
- suspected significant caffeine ingestion
- swallowed cable/plastic/string-like material plus vomiting, anorexia, constipation, or lethargy
